\title{Cycles through 1 for the $qn+1$ map: no cycles with three odd steps, and constraints on longer cycles}
\author{Trevor Hackman}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
For the $qn+1$ map with $q$ an odd integer at least $3$, we prove that $1$ never lies on a cycle with exactly three distinct odd numbers.
This settles the three-step case of a conjecture of Santos that $1$ is periodic only for $q = 5$ and for Mersenne numbers $q = 2^e - 1$.
We found that for every such $q$ at least $2^{22001}$, a lower bound and an upper bound cannot hold at the same time.
Bauer and Bennett (2018) give an irrationality measure whose exponent, about $1.6149$, lies just below the golden ratio, the threshold at which these bounds conflict.
Every smaller $q$ is ruled out by a direct computation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Fix an odd integer $q \ge 3$.
For an odd positive integer $n$, let $v$ be the largest integer such that $2^v$ divides $qn+1$.
The map $C$ sends $n$ to
\[
C(n) = \frac{qn+1}{2^v}.
\]
The orbit of $n$ is the sequence $n$, $C(n)$, $C(C(n))$, \ldots.
When $q = 3$, $C$ is the odd-to-odd or accelerated form of the Collatz map, and the Collatz conjecture, posed by Lothar Collatz in 1937 and still open, asserts that every orbit eventually reaches $1$.
For $q = 3$ the number $1$ is fixed, since $3 \cdot 1 + 1 = 4$.
For larger $q$, heuristic arguments suggest that most orbits grow without bound and that cycles are rare, although no orbit has been proved to diverge.

We say that $1$ is periodic if its orbit returns to $1$, and ask for which $q$ this happens.
We classify the answers by the length $\ell$ of the cycle through $1$, that is, the number of distinct odd numbers on it.

For $\ell = 1$ the cycle is $1 \to 1$, which happens exactly when $q+1$ is a power of $2$, that is $q = 2^e - 1$ (Santos, Proposition~15).
For $\ell = 2$, the only $q$ is $5$, with $1 \to 3 \to 1$ (Santos, Proposition~16; a short proof also follows from Beukers's theorem on $x^2 + D = 2^n$, stated as Theorem~1.8 in Bauer and Bennett (2002)).
Santos conjectured that these are the only cases: $1$ is periodic only for $q = 5$ and $q = 2^e - 1$.
Besides the Mersenne family $1 \to 1$, five cycles of $C$ are known: three at $q = 5$ and two at $q = 181$.
Section~\ref{sec:all-cycles} conjectures that these are all the cycles of $C$, and gives a heuristic.
If so, then for every odd $q \ge 7$ other than $181$ and the Mersenne numbers $q = 2^e - 1$, every orbit of $C$ diverges.

\begin{named}{Theorem 1.}
No odd $q \ge 3$ has $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $3$.
\end{named}

\begin{named}{Outline of Theorem 2 (golden-ratio criterion).}
Fix $\ell \ge 2$, and suppose the roots of $2$ that this length produces have an approximation exponent below the golden ratio.
Then any $q$ with $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$ lies below a bound we can compute.
Checking every candidate up to that bound finds every such $q$, or shows that none exist.
The precise statement is in Section~\ref{sec:longer}.
\end{named}

\begin{named}{Proposition 3 (finiteness through 1).}
For each $\ell \ge 2$, only finitely many odd $q$ have $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$.
\end{named}

\begin{named}{Theorem 4.}
For each $\ell \ge 2$, only finitely many odd $q$ have a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$.
\end{named}

Theorem~4 is ineffective: it rests on the general form of Ridout's theorem in Section~\ref{sec:ineffective}, which allows prime factors of the denominator outside a fixed finite set, and gives no bound on $q$, and no bound depending on a fixed starting value.
(A finiteness result is called effective if its proof gives an explicit bound on any exception, so that a finite computation can find them all, and ineffective if it gives no such bound.)
Theorem~1 is effective and is completed by computation.
Proposition~3 is the case of Theorem~4 in which the cycle visits~$1$.
Its proof, in Section~\ref{sec:ineffective}, uses only the $S$-unit form of Ridout's theorem and Theorem~2.

\section{Definitions and notation}
\label{sec:definitions}

Throughout, $q \ge 3$ is an odd integer and $C$ is the map defined in Section~1.

\topic{The $2$-adic valuation.}
For a nonzero integer $n$, $v_2(n)$ is the largest integer $v$ such that $2^v$ divides $n$.

\topic{Cycles.}
A cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$ consists of $\ell$ distinct odd positive integers.
The smallest element is $x_0$.
Applying $C$ repeatedly, starting at $x_0$, produces
\[
x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_{\ell-1} \to x_0.
\]

\topic{Cycles through 1.}
An $\ell$-odd cycle through $1$ is a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$ with $x_0 = 1$.
It is written
\[
1 = x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_{\ell-1} \to 1.
\]

\topic{Halvings.}
The halving counts $k_1, \ldots, k_\ell$ of the cycle are defined by
\[
q x_{i-1} + 1 = 2^{k_i} x_i
\qquad (1 \le i \le \ell,\ x_\ell = x_0),
\]
so $k_i = v_2(q \cdot x_{i-1} + 1)$.
The total number of halvings is $h = k_1 + \cdots + k_\ell$, and $S_i = k_1 + \cdots + k_i$.

\topic{The window.}
On an $\ell$-odd cycle through $1$ with $h$ halvings in all, write $2^{h/\ell} = q + \delta$, so $\delta$ is the fractional part of $2^{h/\ell}$.
The window is a lower bound and an upper bound on $\delta$.

\topic{Near misses.}
For an element $x_i$ other than $1$ on an $\ell$-odd cycle through $1$, set $n_i = ih - \ell S_i$.
The near miss of $x_i$ is $2^{n_i/\ell}$.
The relative error $\rho_i$ is defined by
\[
x_i = 2^{n_i/\ell}(1 + \rho_i).
\]

\topic{Approximation exponents.}
Let $\theta$ be irrational and let $\mathcal{D}$ be a set of positive integers.
Then $\theta$ has effective approximation exponent $\mu$ with respect to $\mathcal{D}$ when there are explicit constants $c > 0$ and $d_0$ such that
\[
\left|\theta - \frac{p}{d}\right| > c \cdot d^{-\mu}
\]
for every integer $p$ and every $d \in \mathcal{D}$ with $d \ge d_0$.
Write $\mathcal{D}_\ell$ for the set of denominators $2^j$ and $\ell \cdot 2^j$, where $j$ is a nonnegative integer.

\topic{The golden ratio.}
$\varphi = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2$.

\topic{The order of $2$.}
For an odd positive integer $m$, the order of $2$ modulo $m$ is the smallest positive integer $d$ such that $2^d \equiv 1 \pmod{m}$.

\topic{Other conventions.}
An implied constant in $O$, $\ll$, $\gg$, or $\asymp$ depends only on $\ell$.
In a heuristic remark, $\lesssim$ and $\gtrsim$ mean at most or at least roughly, ignoring constants.

\topic{Three odd steps.}
For an $\ell$-odd cycle through $1$ with $\ell = 3$, write the cycle as $1 \to x \to y \to 1$.
The element after $1$ is $x = x_1$.
The element after $x$ is $y = x_2$.
The three steps are
\[
q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x, \qquad qx + 1 = 2^{k_2} y, \qquad qy + 1 = 2^{k_3}.
\]

\section{The structure of a three-step cycle}
\label{sec:structure}

\begin{named}{Lemma 1.}
$k_3 \ge k_1 + 3$, and $y \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The odd elements other than $1$ are at least $3$, so $x \ge 3$ and $y \ge 3$.
The first step is $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$, and therefore $q + 1 \ge 3 \cdot 2^{k_1}$, that is $q \ge 3 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 1$.
The third step is $2^{k_3} = qy + 1$, so
\[
2^{k_3} \ge (3 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 1) \cdot 3 + 1 = 9 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 2.
\]
Since $2^{k_1} \ge 2$, we have $9 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 2 \ge 8 \cdot 2^{k_1} = 2^{k_1 + 3}$, and hence $k_3 \ge k_1 + 3$.
From $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$ we also have $q \equiv -1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
Thus $qy + 1 \equiv 1 - y \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
But $qy + 1 = 2^{k_3}$ is divisible by $2^{k_1}$, because $k_3 \ge k_1$, so $1 - y \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$, that is $y \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 2 (cycle equation).}
\[
2^h = q^3 + q^2 + 2^{k_1} q + 2^{k_1 + k_2}.
\]
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Since $h = k_1 + k_2 + k_3$,
\[
2^h = 2^{k_1 + k_2} \cdot 2^{k_3}.
\]
The third step is $qy + 1 = 2^{k_3}$, so
\[
2^h = 2^{k_1 + k_2}(qy + 1) = 2^{k_1}\bigl(q \cdot 2^{k_2} y + 2^{k_2}\bigr).
\]
The second step is $qx + 1 = 2^{k_2} y$, and therefore
\[
2^h = 2^{k_1}\bigl(q(qx + 1) + 2^{k_2}\bigr) = 2^{k_1}(q^2 x + q + 2^{k_2}) = q^2(2^{k_1} x) + 2^{k_1} q + 2^{k_1 + k_2}.
\]
The first step is $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$, so
\[
2^h = q^2(q + 1) + 2^{k_1} q + 2^{k_1 + k_2} = q^3 + q^2 + 2^{k_1} q + 2^{k_1 + k_2}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 3 (cube window).}
\markstmt{3}{lem:cube-window}
$q^3 < 2^h < (q+1)^3$.
Hence $3$ does not divide $h$ and $q = \lfloor 2^{h/3} \rfloor$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound is immediate from Lemma~2.
Since $2^{k_1 + k_2} = (q(q+1) + 2^{k_1})/y \le (q+1)(3q+1)/9$ by $y \ge 3$, and $2^{k_1} \le (q+1)/3$,
\[
2^h - q^3
\le q^2 + \frac{q(q+1)}{3} + \frac{(q+1)(3q+1)}{9}
= \frac{15q^2 + 7q + 1}{9}
< (q+1)^3 - q^3.
\]
A power of $2$ strictly between consecutive cubes is not a cube, so $3 \nmid h$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 4 ($2$-adic valuation of $x-1$).}
Let $t = v_2(x-1)$ and $s = (x-1)/2^t$.
Then either $t = 2k_1$, or $t < k_1$ and $k_1 = t + v_2(s+1)$, or $t = k_1$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The first step is $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$, so $q = 2^{k_1} x - 1$.

Substitute $q = 2^{k_1} x - 1$ into the second step $2^{k_2} y = qx + 1$:
\[
2^{k_2} y = (2^{k_1} x - 1)x + 1 = 2^{k_1} x^2 - x + 1 = 2^{k_1} x^2 - (x - 1).
\]

Suppose $t > k_1$.
Since $x$ is odd, $v_2(2^{k_1} x^2) = k_1$, while $v_2(x-1) = t > k_1$, so
\[
2^{k_1} x^2 - (x-1) = 2^{k_1}\bigl(x^2 - 2^{t-k_1}s\bigr),
\]
and $x^2 - 2^{t-k_1}s$ is odd, since $x^2$ is odd and $2^{t-k_1}s$ is even.
Thus the $2$-adic valuation of $2^{k_2} y$ is $k_1$. Since $y$ is odd, $k_2 = k_1$, and $y = x^2 - w$ with $w = (x-1)/2^{k_1}$ even.
Substituting $x = 1 + 2^{k_1} w$,
\[
qy + 1 = 2^{k_1} + w(1 - 3 \cdot 2^{k_1}) + 2^{2k_1} w(3 - 2w) + 3 \cdot 2^{3k_1} w^2 + 2^{4k_1} w^3.
\]
The terms $2^{2k_1} w(3 - 2w)$, $3 \cdot 2^{3k_1} w^2$, and $2^{4k_1} w^3$ are divisible by $2^{2k_1 + v_2(w)}$, and $1 - 3 \cdot 2^{k_1}$ is odd, so $v_2\bigl(w(1 - 3 \cdot 2^{k_1})\bigr) = v_2(w)$. If $v_2(w) \ne k_1$, then $v_2(qy + 1) = \min(k_1, v_2(w)) \le k_1$, contradicting Lemma~1.
Hence $v_2(w) = k_1$, that is $t = 2k_1$.

Suppose $t < k_1$.
Then $2^{k_2} y = 2^{k_1} x^2 - 2^t s = 2^t\bigl(2^{k_1-t}x^2 - s\bigr)$.
The factor $2^{k_1-t}x^2 - s$ is odd, since $2^{k_1-t}x^2$ is even and $s$ is odd, so $k_2 = t$, $y = 2^{k_1-t}x^2 - s$, and
\[
qy + 1 = (2^{k_1}-1)\, 2^{k_1-t} x^2 + (1+s) - 2^{k_1} s + 2^{k_1 + t} s y.
\]
Now $2^{k_1} s$ and $2^{k_1 + t} s y$ are divisible by $2^{k_1}$, and $v_2\bigl((2^{k_1}-1)\, 2^{k_1-t} x^2\bigr) = k_1 - t$, since $2^{k_1}-1$ and $x$ are odd. If $v_2(s+1) \ne k_1 - t$, then $v_2(qy + 1) < k_1$, contradicting Lemma~1.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 5.}
$2^{k_1} \le 2(x-1)$ and $x > \sqrt{q/2}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
In the three cases of Lemma~4: $2^{2k_1} = 2^t \le x - 1$; or $2^{k_1} = 2^t \cdot 2^{v_2(s+1)} \le 2^t(s+1) = (x-1) + 2^t \le 2(x-1)$; or $2^{k_1} = 2^t \le x - 1$.
The first step is $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$, so
\[
q + 1 \le 2x(x-1) = 2x^2 - 2x.
\]
Hence $q < 2x^2$, that is $x > \sqrt{q/2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 6 (consecutive triples).}
Let $a \to b \to c$ be consecutive elements of any orbit of $C$, with $qa + 1 = 2^J b$ and $qb + 1 = 2^K c$.
Then
\[
2^K ac - 2^J b^2 = a - b,
\]
so if $a \ne b$ then $|a - b| \ge 2^{\min(J,K)}$.
Consequently, if $J \le K$ then $b > q$ when $b < a$, and $b^2 > qa$ when $b > a$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Substitute $q = (2^K c - 1)/b$ into $qa + 1 = 2^J b$ and multiply by $b$.
If $J \le K$, then $|a - b| \ge 2^J = (qa + 1)/b$; if $b > a$ this gives $b^2 > b(b-a) \ge qa + 1$, and if $b < a$ it gives $b(a-b) \ge qa + 1$, so $b > q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Corollary 7.}
$y > x$ and $y^2 > qx$.
In particular $2^{k_2} < q + 1$, so $2^{k_1 + k_2} \le 2^{k_1} q$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~6 to $x \to y \to 1$, so $J = k_2$ and $K = k_3$.
If $k_3 < k_2$, then $|x - y| \ge 2^{k_3} = qy + 1 > y$, which forces $x > qy > q$, impossible as $x \le (q+1)/2$.
So $k_2 \le k_3$.
If $y < x$, Lemma~6 gives $y > q > x$, a contradiction.
Hence $y > x$ and $y^2 > y(y-x) \ge qx + 1$.
Finally $2^{k_2} = (qx + 1)/y < (qx + 1)/x < q + 1$, and $2^{k_2}$ is even while $q$ is odd.
\end{proof}

\section{The window and the near miss for $x$}
\label{sec:window}

\begin{named}{Lemma 8 (the window).}
\markstmt{8}{lem:window}
Let $N = 27 \cdot 2^h - (3q+1)^3$.
Then
\[
N = 9q(3 \cdot 2^{k_1}-1) + 27 \cdot 2^{k_1 + k_2} - 1,
\qquad
0 < N < 54 \cdot 2^{k_1} q,
\qquad
0 < \delta - \frac{1}{3} \le \frac{N}{81q^2} < \frac{2^{k_1 + 1}}{3q}.
\]
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The formula for $N$ follows from Lemma~2 by expanding $(3q+1)^3$.
Since $2^{k_1} \ge 2$, $N \ge 45q - 1 > 0$, and $2^{k_1 + k_2} \le 2^{k_1} q$ (Corollary~7) gives $N < 54 \cdot 2^{k_1} q$.
Finally $N = 27\bigl[(q+\delta)^3 - (q + 1/3)^3\bigr]$; as $N > 0$ we have $\delta > 1/3$, and then $N \ge 81q^2(\delta - 1/3)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 9 (near miss for $x$).}
\markstmt{9}{lem:near-x}
Let $f = h - 3k_1$.
Then $3 \nmid f$, and
\[
\bigl|x^3 - 2^f\bigr| \le \frac{3.2\, x^2}{2^{k_1}},
\qquad
\bigl|x - 2^{f/3}\bigr| \le \frac{1.6}{2^{k_1}}.
\]
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Since $2^{3k_1} x^3 = (q+1)^3$, Lemma~2 gives $2^{3k_1} x^3 - 2^h = E$ with $E = 2q^2 + (3 - 2^{k_1})q + 1 - 2^{k_1 + k_2}$.
Using $q < 2^{k_1} x$, $|3 - 2^{k_1}| \le 2^{k_1}$ and $2^{k_1 + k_2} \le 2^{k_1} q < 2^{2k_1} x$, we get $|E| \le 2 \cdot 2^{2k_1} x^2 + 2 \cdot 2^{2k_1} x + 1 \le 3.2 \cdot 2^{2k_1} x^2$ for $x \ge 3$.
Dividing by $2^{3k_1}$ gives the first bound, and $f \equiv h \pmod{3}$.
Write $t = 2^{f/3}$.
If $|x - t| \ge 1$, then $|x^3 - t^3| \ge 3x^2 - 3x + 1 \ge 2x^2 > 3.2 x^2/2^{k_1}$, a contradiction since $2^{k_1} \ge 2$.
So $|x - t| < 1$, hence $t > x - 1 \ge 2x/3$ and $x^2 + xt + t^2 > 2x^2$, which gives the second bound.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of Theorem 1}
\label{sec:proof}

We use the following effective irrationality measure, which is the inequality obtained in the proof of Theorem~3 of Bauer and Bennett (2018) from their inequality~(21), Lemma~7 and Proposition~8, before they round it to $2^{-1.62k}$ in~(7).

\begin{named}{Theorem (Bauer--Bennett).}
Let $j \ge 11000$, $r \in \{1, 2\}$, $s \in \{1, 3\}$ and $n = 3j + r$.
For every integer $p$,
\[
\left| 2^{r/3} - \frac{p}{s\, 2^j} \right|
\ge
\left| 1 - \frac{p}{s\, 2^{n/3}} \right|
>
2^{-5} \cdot 16166467234^{-(n+77)/63}.
\]
\end{named}

The factor $2^{-5}$ is $1/(1.26 \cdot 2^{14/3})$ from their inequality~(21); the paper's displayed intermediate step omits it, though it is absorbed in their final statement.
Per unit of $j$ the exponent is $\mu = 3 \log_2(16166467234)/63 \approx 1.61487$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~1]
Suppose $q \ge 2^{22001}$.
Write $a = k_1$, $h = 3m + r$ and $f = h - 3a = 3j' + r'$, where $r, r' \in \{1, 2\}$ by Lemmas~\ref{lem:cube-window} and~\ref{lem:near-x}.
Lemma~5 gives $x > \sqrt{q/2} \ge 2^{11000}$.
Since $q$ is odd, $a \ge 1$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:near-x} gives $|x - 2^{f/3}| \le 0.8$.
Also $2^{f/3} \le 2^{j'+2/3}$.
If $j' \le 10999$, then $x \le 2^{10999+2/3} + 0.8$.
Now $(8/5)^3 = 512/125 > 4$, so $2^{2/3} < 8/5$, and $2^{11000} - (8/5)\,2^{10999} = (2/5)\,2^{10999} > 0.8$.
Thus $x < 2^{11000}$, a contradiction, and $j' \ge 11000$.
Likewise $(5/4)^3 = 125/64 < 2$, so $2^{1/3} > 5/4$ and $2^{j'}/4 > 0.8$, which gives $x \ge 2^{j'+1/3} - 0.8 > 2^{j'} + 2^{j'}/4 - 0.8 > 2^{j'}$.
Hence $x \ge 2^{j'}+1$ and $q = 2^a x - 1 \ge 2^{a+j'} + 2^a - 1 \ge 2^{a+j'} + 1$.
From Lemma~\ref{lem:cube-window}, $2^h > q^3$, so $2^{h/3} > q > 2^{a+j'}$ and $m + r/3 > a + j'$.
As $r \le 2$, this forces $m \ge a + j' \ge 11000$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The window.}
Apply Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) with $s = 3$, $p = 3q + 1$, $j = m$.
Here $1 - p/(3 \cdot 2^{h/3}) = (\delta - 1/3)/2^{h/3}$, so $\delta - 1/3 > 2^m \cdot 2^{-5} \cdot 16166467234^{-(h+77)/63}$.
With Lemma~\ref{lem:window} and $q \ge 2^m$,
\[
2^a > 1.5 \cdot 2^{2m-5} \cdot 16166467234^{-(h+77)/63}.
\]
\item \textbf{The near miss for $x$.}
Apply Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) with $s = 1$, $p = x$, $j = j'$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:near-x}, $|1 - x/2^{f/3}| \le 1.6/(2^a \cdot 2^{f/3})$, so
\[
2^a < 1.6 \cdot 2^{5 - f/3} \cdot 16166467234^{(f+77)/63}.
\]
\item \textbf{Combining.}
With $h = f + 3a$, $m \ge a + j'$ and $j' \ge (f-2)/3$, step~1 gives $a > 0.20878\, f - 76.8$ and step~2 gives $a < 0.20496\, f + 47.2$.
These are incompatible once $f \ge 32{,}461$, that is $j' \ge 10{,}820$.
\end{enumerate}
So any $3$-odd cycle through $1$ has $q < 2^{22001}$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:cube-window} each $h$ gives a single candidate $q = \lfloor 2^{h/3} \rfloor$, and checking every $h < 66{,}100$ directly (Appendix~\ref{app:computations}) finds no cycle.
\end{proof}

\topic{Why the golden ratio appears.}
The proof squeezes $x$ between two bounds, both governed by the exponent $\mu$ in Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The window keeps $x$ small.}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:window}, $2^{h/3}$ exceeds $q + 1/3$ by about $1/(3x)$, so $(3q+1)/(3 \cdot 2^m)$ approximates $2^{r/3}$ with error about $1/(xq)$.
Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) forbids errors below about $q^{-\mu}$, so $x \lesssim q^{\mu-1}$.
\item \textbf{The near miss for $x$ keeps $x$ large.}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:near-x}, $x/2^{j'}$ approximates $2^{r'/3}$ with error about $1/q$, where $2^{j'} \asymp x$.
Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) forbids errors below about $x^{-\mu}$, so $x \gtrsim q^{1/\mu}$.
\end{itemize}
Both bounds can hold only if $1/\mu \le \mu - 1$, that is $\mu^2 - \mu - 1 \ge 0$, whose positive root is $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2$.
With $\mu = 1.61487$ the bounds read $q^{0.6192} \lesssim x \lesssim q^{0.6149}$, which are incompatible once $q$ is large; the explicit constants in steps~1 to~3 make this precise for $q \ge 2^{22001}$.
The two bounds are tied together by $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$: a large $x$ makes the window's approximation too good, while a small $x$ forces $2^{k_1}$ to be large and makes $x$'s own approximation too good.

The threshold $\varphi$ comes from this argument; the exponent comes from Bauer and Bennett, who needed only an exponent below $2$ for their own purposes.
Their stated exponent $1.62$ lies just above $\varphi$ and would leave room for $x$; the unrounded $1.61487$ lies just below it.

\section{Longer cycles}
\label{sec:longer}

\begin{named}{Lemma 10 (the $\ell$-th power window).}
\markstmt{10}{lem:power-window}
For an $\ell$-odd cycle through $1$,
\[
2^h = (q+1) \prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1} \left( q + \frac{1}{x_i} \right).
\]
If $\ell \ge 2$, then $q^\ell < 2^h < (q+1)^\ell$, $\ell \nmid h$ and $q = \lfloor 2^{h/\ell} \rfloor$.
Moreover, with $\sigma = \sum 1/x_i$, we have $\delta - 1/\ell = \sigma/\ell + O(1/q)$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Multiply the relations $2^{k_i} = (q \cdot x_{i-1} + 1)/x_i$ around the cycle.
The product on the right is empty when $\ell = 1$, and then $2^h = q + 1$.
If $\ell \ge 2$, the product contains a factor $q + 1/x_i$ with $x_i \ge 3$, and every such factor lies strictly between $q$ and $q+1$.
Hence $q^\ell < 2^h < (q+1)^\ell$.
A power of $2$ strictly between consecutive $\ell$-th powers is not an $\ell$-th power, so $\ell \nmid h$, and $q = \lfloor 2^{h/\ell} \rfloor$.
Comparing $(q+\delta)^\ell$ with the expanded product gives the last statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 11 (every element is a near miss).}
\markstmt{11}{lem:near-misses}
For $1 \le i \le \ell - 1$,
\[
x_i \, 2^{S_i} = q^i (1 + \varepsilon_i),
\qquad
\varepsilon_i = \frac{1}{q} + \sum_{u=1}^{i-1} \frac{1 + \varepsilon_u}{q x_u}.
\]
Consequently $x_i = 2^{n_i/\ell}(1 + \rho_i)$ with $n_i = ih - \ell S_i$, and for $q \ge \ell$,
\[
-\frac{i}{q+1} < \rho_i \le \frac{2i+1}{3q},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
|\rho_i| \le \frac{\ell - 1}{q}.
\]
If $\ell$ divides $n_i$, then $x_i \ge q/(\ell-1) - 1$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The formula follows by induction from $q \cdot x_{i-1} + 1 = 2^{k_i} \cdot x_i$, using $2^{S_u}/q^{u+1} = (1 + \varepsilon_u)/(q \cdot x_u)$.
Then $1 + \rho_i = x_i \cdot 2^{S_i} / 2^{ih/\ell} = \bigl(q/(q+\delta)\bigr)^i (1 + \varepsilon_i)$ with $0 < \delta < 1$.
Since $x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}$ are distinct odd numbers at least $3$, their reciprocals sum to at most $(i-1)/3$, so by induction $\varepsilon_i \le 1/q + (2/q)(i-1)/3 = (2i+1)/(3q)$, which is at most $1$ for $q \ge \ell$.
Also $1 \ge \bigl(q/(q+\delta)\bigr)^i > \bigl(1 - 1/(q+1)\bigr)^i \ge 1 - i/(q+1)$.
Together these give the bounds on $\rho_i$, and both sides are at most $(\ell-1)/q$ in absolute value because $i \le \ell - 1$.
If $\ell \mid n_i$, the target $2^{n_i/\ell}$ is a power of $2$ and $x_i$ is odd, so $|x_i - 2^{n_i/\ell}| \ge 1$ and hence $|\rho_i| \ge 2^{-n_i/\ell} \ge (1 - |\rho_i|)/x_i$, which gives $x_i \ge 1/|\rho_i| - 1 \ge q/(\ell-1) - 1$.
\end{proof}

The outline in the introduction is the case $R = \{1, \ldots, \ell-1\}$.

\begin{named}{Theorem 2.}
Let $\ell \ge 2$, let $R \subseteq \{1, \ldots, \ell-1\}$, and let $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2$.
Suppose that there are real numbers $0 < \mu < \varphi$, $c > 0$ and $j_0$ such that
\[
\left| 2^{r/\ell} - \frac{p}{s \cdot 2^j} \right| > c \cdot 2^{-\mu j}
\]
for all $r \in R$, $s \in \{1, \ell\}$, $p \in \mathbb{Z}$ and integers $j \ge j_0$.
Then there is a number $Q$, computable from $\ell$, $\mu$, $c$ and $j_0$, with the following property.
If $q > Q$ is odd and $1 = x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_{\ell-1} \to 1$ is a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$, with $q \cdot x_{i-1} + 1 = 2^{k_i} \cdot x_i$ for $1 \le i \le \ell$ (where $x_\ell = 1$), then some multiple of $h = k_1 + \cdots + k_\ell$ has a nonzero residue modulo $\ell$ outside $R$.
\end{named}

For $R = \{1, \ldots, \ell-1\}$ the conclusion is that no $q > Q$ has $1$ on a cycle of length $\ell$.
For $\ell = 4$ and $R = \{2\}$, the multiples of $h$ avoid the residues $1$ and $3$ exactly when $h$ is even, so the conclusion is that $h$ is odd.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~2]
Suppose that every multiple of $h$ has its nonzero residues modulo $\ell$ in $R$.
Write $h = \ell m + r$ with $r \in R$, and assume $q \ge 2(\ell-1)$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:near-misses} gives $|\rho_i| \le 1/2$.
The window uses $2^{r/\ell}$, and $x_i$ uses $2^{n_i/\ell}$ with $n_i \equiv ir \pmod{\ell}$, so every root below has its residue in $R$.
Set
\[
A = 2^{1/\ell - 1}\Bigl(\frac{c}{2(\ell-1)}\Bigr)^{1/\mu},
\qquad
\gamma = 1 - \mu + \frac1\mu > 0,
\]
the last because $\mu^2 - \mu - 1 < 0$.

If $q < 2^{j_0+1}$, stop: this is already a bound.
If $q \ge 2^{j_0+1}$, then $2^m = (q+\delta)/2^{r/\ell} > q/2 \ge 2^{j_0}$, so $m \ge j_0$ and the hypothesis applies to the window.
\[
\Bigl|\delta - \frac1\ell\Bigr|
= 2^m \Bigl| 2^{r/\ell} - \frac{\ell q + 1}{\ell \cdot 2^m} \Bigr|
> c \cdot 2^{(1-\mu)m}.
\]
If $\mu > 1$ then $2^m < q+1$ gives $|\delta - 1/\ell| > c(q+1)^{1-\mu} \ge c\, 2^{1-\mu} q^{1-\mu}$.
If $\mu \le 1$ then $2^m > q/2$ gives $|\delta - 1/\ell| > c(q/2)^{1-\mu} \ge c/2$.

For each $x_i$ with $\ell \nmid n_i$, write $n_i = \ell j + r'$ and $x_i/2^j = 2^{r'/\ell}(1+\rho_i)$, so $|2^{r'/\ell} - x_i/2^j| = 2^{r'/\ell}|\rho_i|$.
Also $x_i/3 \le 2^j \le 2x_i$.
If $j < j_0$, then $x_i < 3 \cdot 2^{j_0}$.
The finitely many triples $(r', j, x)$ with $r' \in R$, $0 \le j < j_0$ and $x$ odd, $3 \le x < 3 \cdot 2^{j_0}$, each have a positive $|\rho| = |x \cdot 2^{-j-r'/\ell} - 1|$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:near-misses} forces $q \le (\ell-1)/|\rho|$.
Let $Q_0$ be the maximum of those finitely many values.
If $j \ge j_0$, the hypothesis and $|\rho_i| \le (\ell-1)/q$ give $c \cdot 2^{-\mu j} < 2(\ell-1)/q$, so
\[
2^j > \Bigl(\frac{cq}{2(\ell-1)}\Bigr)^{1/\mu},
\qquad
x_i \ge 2^{1/\ell-1}\, 2^j \ge A\, q^{1/\mu}.
\]
If $\ell \mid n_i$, then $x_i \ge q/(\ell-1) - 1 \ge q/(2(\ell-1))$.
Thus every $x_i$ is at least $A q^{1/\mu}$ once $q$ is large enough that $A q^{1/\mu} \le q/(2(\ell-1))$, and
\[
\sigma \le (\ell-1) A^{-1} q^{-1/\mu}.
\]

Expanding the product in Lemma~\ref{lem:power-window},
\[
\Bigl(1 + \frac{\delta}{q}\Bigr)^\ell
= \Bigl(1 + \frac1q\Bigr)\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\Bigl(1 + \frac1{qx_i}\Bigr).
\]
The product is $1 + \sigma/q + \rho$ with $0 < \rho \le (\sigma/q)^2 e^{\sigma/q}$.
Comparing with the binomial expansion of the left side, and using $\sigma \le (\ell-1)/3$ and $q \ge \ell$,
\[
\Bigl|\delta - \frac1\ell - \frac{\sigma}{\ell}\Bigr|
\le \frac{\sigma}{\ell q} + \frac{\rho(q+1)}{\ell} + \frac{\ell\, e^{\ell/q}}{q}
\le \frac{4\ell}{q}.
\]
Hence $|\delta - 1/\ell| \le A^{-1} q^{-1/\mu} + 4\ell/q$.
For $\mu \ge 1$ the second term is at most $4\ell\, q^{-1/\mu}$, so the right-hand side is at most $(A^{-1} + 4\ell)\, q^{-1/\mu}$.

If $\mu > 1$, the two bounds on $|\delta - 1/\ell|$ conflict once $c\, 2^{1-\mu} q^{1-\mu} > (A^{-1} + 4\ell)\, q^{-1/\mu}$, that is
\[
q > Q_* := \Biggl( \frac{A^{-1} + 4\ell}{c\, 2^{1-\mu}} \Biggr)^{1/\gamma}.
\]
If $\mu \le 1$, they conflict once $c/2 > (A^{-1} + 4\ell)\, q^{-1/\mu}$, that is $q > \bigl(2(A^{-1} + 4\ell)/c\bigr)^\mu$; use this as $Q_*$ instead.

Take $Q = \max\bigl(2(\ell-1),\ 2^{j_0+1},\ Q_0,\ Q_*\bigr)$.
\end{proof}

For $\ell = 3$, Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) supplies the hypothesis of Theorem~2 with $R = \{1, 2\}$, $\mu \approx 1.61487$ and $j_0 = 11000$.
So Theorem~1 is the case $\ell = 3$ of Theorem~2; Section~\ref{sec:proof} carries out this case with explicit constants.

\begin{named}{Corollary 12.}
No odd $q$ has $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $4$ whose total number of halvings is $\equiv 2 \pmod{4}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
This is Theorem~2 with $\ell = 4$ and $R = \{2\}$, with explicit constants.
Write $h = 4m + 2$.
Then $n_1 \equiv n_3 \equiv 2$ and $n_2 \equiv 0 \pmod{4}$, so only $\sqrt{2}$ and powers of $2$ occur, and the window approximates $\sqrt{2}$ by $(4q+1)/2^{m+2}$.
Bauer and Bennett's Corollary~1.6 (2002) gives $|\sqrt{2} - p/2^j| > 2^{-1.48 j}$ for every $j > 2$ with $j \notin \{3, 7, 8\}$.
\begin{itemize}
\item By Lemma~\ref{lem:near-misses}, $x_2 \ge q/3.1$, and $x_1$, $x_3$ are near $\sqrt{2} \cdot 2^j$, so $4.3/q > (x/1.41)^{-1.48}$, giving $x_1, x_3 \ge (0.387q)^{0.6757}$.
\item The measure gives $|\delta - 1/4| > 0.152(q+1)^{-0.48}$, while expanding the product in Lemma~\ref{lem:power-window} explicitly gives $|\delta - 1/4| \le \sigma/4 + 1.75/q \le 0.95\, q^{-0.6757} + 2.53/q$.
\end{itemize}
For $q \ge 10^5$ every exponent involved exceeds $8$, so Corollary~1.6 applies, and the two bounds are then incompatible (the inequalities already conflict from $q = 2 \times 10^4$). The search over $h$ covers $\ell = 4$ for $q < 2^{25{,}000}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Corollary 13.}
Let $\ell = 2^a$ with $a \ge 2$.
Only finitely many odd $q$ have $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$ whose total number of halvings is $\equiv \ell/2 \pmod{\ell}$, and a bound is computable from Bauer and Bennett's Corollary~1.6 (2002).
For $\ell = 4$ every such $q$ is ruled out by Corollary~12.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Write $h = \ell m + \ell/2$.
Every multiple of $h$ is congruent to $0$ or $\ell/2$ modulo $\ell$, so the only root that occurs is $2^{1/2}$.
A denominator $s \cdot 2^j$ with $s \in \{1, \ell\}$ is a power of $2$, because $\ell$ is.
Bauer and Bennett's Corollary~1.6 (2002) therefore gives the hypothesis of Theorem~2 with $R = \{\ell/2\}$, $\mu = 1.48 < \varphi$, $c = 1$ and $j_0 = 9$: the finitely many excluded exponents $j \in \{1, 2, 3, 7, 8\}$ contribute only a constant factor depending on $\ell$.
The bound is then the $Q$ in the proof of Theorem~2 for these values.
The case $\ell = 4$ is Corollary~12.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Corollary 14.}
Let $\ell = 3 \cdot 2^a$ with $a \ge 1$.
Only finitely many odd $q$ have $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$ whose total number of halvings is $\equiv \ell/3$ or $2\ell/3 \pmod{\ell}$, and a bound is computable from Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett).
The length $3$ is Theorem~1, which rules out every such $q$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Write $h = \ell m + r_0$ with $r_0 \in \{\ell/3,\, 2\ell/3\}$.
Every multiple of $h$ is congruent to $0$, $\ell/3$, or $2\ell/3$ modulo $\ell$, so the only irrational roots that occur are $2^{1/3}$ and $2^{2/3}$.
In the proof of Theorem~2 the window fraction is $(\ell q + 1)/(\ell \cdot 2^m)$.
Its numerator is coprime to $\ell$, and $\ell \cdot 2^m = 3 \cdot 2^{m+a}$, so this is a fraction of the shape in Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) with $s = 3$ and exponent $m+a$.
Each near miss is $x_i/2^j$ with $x_i$ odd, so Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) applies with $s = 1$.
Both give the exponent $\mu = 3\log_2(16166467234)/63 \approx 1.61487 < \varphi$ once the exponent in Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) is at least $11000$.
The shift by $a$, and the fixed factors in Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett), contribute only a constant depending on $\ell$.
The fractions used in the proof of Theorem~2 therefore satisfy its hypothesis with $R = \{\ell/3,\, 2\ell/3\}$, this $\mu$, a $c > 0$ read from Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett), and $j_0 = 11000$.
The bound is then the $Q$ in the proof of Theorem~2 for these values.
The length $3$ is Theorem~1.
\end{proof}

\section{Ineffective finiteness}
\label{sec:ineffective}

\begin{named}{Ridout's theorem (1958).}
Let $\alpha$ be an algebraic irrational, let $S$ be a finite set of primes, and let $\varepsilon > 0$.
Only finitely many reduced fractions $p/Q$ with $Q > 0$ have every prime factor of $Q$ in $S$ and satisfy
\[
\left| \alpha - \frac{p}{Q} \right| < Q^{-1-\varepsilon}.
\]
The same theorem has the general form: writing $\lvert n \rvert_p = p^{-v_p(n)}$ for a nonzero integer $n$, only finitely many rationals $P/Q$ in lowest terms satisfy
\[
Q \bigl| Q\alpha - P \bigr| \prod_{p \in S} \lvert Q \rvert_p < \max\bigl(\lvert P \rvert, Q\bigr)^{-\varepsilon}.
\]
\end{named}

The first statement is the $S$-unit form.
If every prime factor of $Q$ lies in $S$, then $\prod_{p \in S} \lvert Q \rvert_p = 1/Q$ and the left side of the general form equals $\lvert Q\alpha - P \rvert$.
Whenever $\lvert P \rvert \le Q$, this is the first form.
The general form allows prime factors of $Q$ outside $S$.
Such a prime does not appear in the product, and it contributes its full size to the height $\max(\lvert P \rvert, Q)$.
Neither form gives a bound on the exceptional fractions.
Since $\alpha$ is irrational, the finitely many fractions that meet the general form for a fixed $\alpha$ lie at a positive distance from $\alpha$, and these distances have a positive minimum.
This is why Theorem~4 and Proposition~3, which use them in place of an effective measure, give no bound on $q$.

\begin{named}{Proposition 3 (finiteness through 1).}
For each $\ell \ge 2$, only finitely many odd $q$ have $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$.
\end{named}

This is the case of Theorem~4 in which the cycle visits~$1$.
The argument below uses only the $S$-unit form of Ridout's theorem and Theorem~2.

\begin{proof}
Let $r \in \{1, \ldots, \ell-1\}$ and let $S$ consist of $2$ and the prime factors of $\ell$.
Then $2^{r/\ell}$ is an algebraic irrational.
A fraction $p/(s \cdot 2^j)$ with $s \in \{1, \ell\}$, written in lowest terms, has every prime factor of its denominator in $S$.
Ridout's theorem therefore gives, for any $\varepsilon > 0$,
\[
\left| 2^{r/\ell} - \frac{p}{s \cdot 2^j} \right| > (s \cdot 2^j)^{-1-\varepsilon}
\]
for all but finitely many such fractions.
The proof of Theorem~2 runs with this inequality in place of an effective measure, and with $\mu = 1 + \varepsilon < \varphi$.
Each exceptional fraction has a fixed nonzero distance from $2^{r/\ell}$.
For an element $x_i$ this fixes $x_i$ and its relative error, which Lemma~\ref{lem:near-misses} allows only for bounded $q$, and for the window it is incompatible with $|\delta - 1/\ell| \to 0$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Proposition~3 fails for $\ell = 1$.}
Evidently, $q = 2^e - 1$ gives the cycle $1 \to 1$ for every $e \ge 2$.
Mathematically, for $\ell \ge 2$ an odd element other than $1$ puts a factor strictly between $q$ and $q+1$, so $\ell$ does not divide $h$ and $2^{r/\ell}$ is irrational.
Ridout's theorem then applies.
For $\ell = 1$ that product is empty, so the same identity becomes $2^h = q + 1$, and the three conclusions of Lemma~\ref{lem:power-window} fail.
The root is the integer $2^h$, and Ridout's theorem does not apply.

Theorems~1 and~2 and Proposition~3 concern cycles through $1$.
The same map $C$ may have cycles that never visit $1$.
Theorem~4 is the finiteness statement for every cycle of each length at least~$2$.
Its proof uses the general form of Ridout's theorem, through the two lemmas below.

\begin{named}{Lemma 15 (the window for any cycle).}
\markstmt{15}{lem:any-window}
Let $a = x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_{\ell-1} \to a$ be a cycle of length $\ell \ge 2$, with $a$ its smallest element, and let $\sigma = \sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1} 1/x_i$.
Then
\[
2^h = \prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(q + \frac{1}{x_i}\right),
\]
so $q^\ell < 2^h < (q+1)^\ell$, and in particular $\ell \nmid h$ and $q = \lfloor 2^{h/\ell} \rfloor$.
Writing $2^{h/\ell} = q + \delta$,
\[
e^{-\ell/q} \le \frac{\ell\,\delta}{\sigma} \le e^{\ell/q}.
\]
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Multiplying $2^{k_i} x_i = q\, x_{i-1} + 1$ around the cycle gives the product.
Each factor lies in $(q,\, q+1]$, with equality to $q+1$ only for an element equal to $1$.
At most one element is $1$, and $\ell \ge 2$, so the product is strictly larger than $q^\ell$ and strictly smaller than $(q+1)^\ell$.
A power of $2$ strictly between consecutive $\ell$-th powers is not an $\ell$-th power, so $\ell \nmid h$, and $q = \lfloor 2^{h/\ell} \rfloor$.
In particular $0 < \delta < 1$.

The binomial theorem gives
\[
\ell q^{\ell-1}\delta \le (q+\delta)^\ell - q^\ell \le \ell q^{\ell-1}\delta\Bigl(1 + \frac{\delta}{q}\Bigr)^{\ell-1}.
\]
Dividing the binomial expansion by $\ell q^{\ell-1}\delta$, the coefficient of $(\delta/q)^k$ is $\binom{\ell}{k+1}/\ell = \binom{\ell-1}{k}/(k+1) \le \binom{\ell-1}{k}$, and $\sum_{k \ge 0}\binom{\ell-1}{k}(\delta/q)^k = (1 + \delta/q)^{\ell-1}$.
Since $\delta < 1$, this factor is at most $e^{(\ell-1)/q} \le e^{\ell/q}$.

The same difference equals $\sum_{j \ge 1} e_j q^{\ell-j}$, where $e_j$ is the $j$-th elementary symmetric function of the reciprocals $1/x_i$.
Thus $e_1 = \sigma$, and the right side is at least $q^{\ell-1}\sigma$.
In the expansion of $\sigma^j$, each product of $j$ distinct reciprocals occurs $j!$ times, so $e_j \le \sigma^j/j!$.
Hence the right side is at most
\[
\sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{\sigma^j}{j!}\, q^{\ell-j} = q^{\ell-1} \sum_{m \ge 0} \frac{\sigma^{m+1}}{(m+1)!\, q^m} \le q^{\ell-1}\sigma\, e^{\sigma/q},
\]
where the last step uses $(m+1)! \ge m!$.
Every element is at least $1$, so $\sigma \le \ell$ and $e^{\sigma/q} \le e^{\ell/q}$.
Comparing the two sides gives $\ell\delta \le \sigma\, e^{\ell/q}$ and $\ell\delta\, e^{\ell/q} \ge \sigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma 16 (three approximations).}
\markstmt{16}{lem:three-approx}
In the setting of Lemma~\ref{lem:any-window}, write $h = \ell m + r$ with $0 < r < \ell$.
Let $\theta_i = 2^{ir/\ell}$ for $1 \le i \le \ell-1$, let $\theta = \theta_1$, and let $y = \min(x_1, \ldots, x_{\ell-1})$.
Suppose $q \ge 4\ell$.
Then $2^m > q/2$, and:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $\lvert \theta - q/2^m \rvert = \delta/2^m$.
\item For $1 \le i \le \ell-1$, the rational number $F_i = x_i/(a \cdot 2^{im - S_i})$ satisfies $\lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert \le 2i\theta_i/(aq)$.
\item The rational number $W = (\ell aq + 1)/(\ell a \cdot 2^m)$ satisfies $\lvert \theta - W \rvert \le 2(1/y + 2\ell/q)/q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Since $2^m \theta = q + \delta$ and $\theta = 2^{r/\ell} < 2$, one has $2^m = (q+\delta)/\theta > q/2$, and part~1 follows.

The relation $q\, x_{i-1} + 1 = 2^{k_i} x_i$ gives $x_i\, 2^{S_i} = a \prod_{u < i}(q + 1/x_u)$ by induction on $i$, so
\[
F_i = \frac{1}{2^{im}}\prod_{u < i}\Bigl(q + \frac{1}{x_u}\Bigr), \qquad \theta_i = \frac{(q+\delta)^i}{2^{im}}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:any-window} gives $\delta \le (\sigma/\ell)\, e^{\ell/q}$.
Every element is at least $a$, so $\sigma \le \ell/a$ and $\delta \le c/a$ with $c = e^{\ell/q} \le e^{1/4}$.
Each factor $q + 1/x_u$ and the number $q+\delta$ therefore lie in $[q,\, q + c/a]$, and both numerators lie in $[q^i,\, (q + c/a)^i]$.
Their difference is at most
\[
\Bigl(q + \frac{c}{a}\Bigr)^i - q^i = q^i\Bigl(\Bigl(1 + \frac{c}{aq}\Bigr)^i - 1\Bigr) \le \frac{ic}{a}\, q^{i-1}\, e^{ic/(aq)},
\]
where $e^z - 1 \le z\, e^z$.
Dividing by $2^{im} = (q+\delta)^i/\theta_i \ge q^i/\theta_i$ gives $\lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert \le (ic\theta_i/(aq))\, e^{ic/(aq)}$.
Here $i \le \ell - 1$ and $aq \ge 4\ell$, so $ic/(aq) < c/4$.
For $0 < z \le 1/3$ and $n \ge 2$, one has $n! \ge 2^{n-1}$, so $z^n/n! \le z^n/2^{n-1}$ and $\sum_{n \ge 2} z^n/2^{n-1} = z^2/(2-z)$.
Thus $e^{1/4} < 1 + 1/4 + (1/16)/(7/4) = 9/7 < 4/3$ and $e^{1/3} < 1 + 1/3 + 1/15 = 7/5$.
Since $c \le e^{1/4} < 4/3$, one has $c/4 < 1/3$ and $c\, e^{c/4} < (4/3) \cdot (7/5) = 28/15 < 2$.
Thus $\lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert \le 2i\theta_i/(aq)$.

For part~3,
\[
\bigl\lvert \theta - W \bigr\rvert = \frac{1}{2^m}\Bigl\lvert \delta - \frac{1}{\ell a} \Bigr\rvert.
\]
Write $\delta - 1/(\ell a) = (\sigma - 1/a)/\ell + (\delta - \sigma/\ell)$.
The other elements are at least $y$, so $0 \le \sigma - 1/a \le (\ell-1)/y$ and $(\sigma - 1/a)/\ell \le 1/y$.
From the two bounds in Lemma~\ref{lem:any-window}, with $z = \ell/q$,
\[
\frac{\sigma}{\ell} - \delta \le \frac{\sigma}{\ell}\bigl(1 - e^{-z}\bigr), \qquad \delta - \frac{\sigma}{\ell} \le \frac{\sigma}{\ell}\bigl(e^z - 1\bigr).
\]
The inequality $1 - e^{-z} \le e^z - 1 \le z\, e^z$ and $\sigma \le \ell$ give $\lvert \delta - \sigma/\ell \rvert \le (\ell/q)\, e^{\ell/q}$.
Since $q \ge 4\ell$, this is at most $(\ell/q)\, e^{1/4} < 2\ell/q$.
Therefore $\lvert \delta - 1/(\ell a) \rvert \le 1/y + 2\ell/q$.
Dividing by $2^m > q/2$ gives part~3.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Theorem 4.}
For each $\ell \ge 2$, only finitely many odd $q$ have a cycle of $C$ of length $\ell$.
\end{named}

The argument does not produce a bound on $q$, and it does not produce a bound depending on a fixed odd start.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~4]
Fix $\ell \ge 2$, and set $\varepsilon = 1/(10\ell)$.
Let $S$ consist of $2$ and the prime factors of $\ell$, and let $\Theta$ be the set of real numbers $2^{t/\ell}$ with $1 \le t \le \ell(\ell-1)$ and $\ell \nmid t$.
Each element of $\Theta$ is an algebraic irrational.
Apply the general form of Ridout's theorem to each $\theta' \in \Theta$, with this $S$ and $\varepsilon$.
Only finitely many fractions meet the inequality, and none equals $\theta'$, so there is an $\eta > 0$ such that every such fraction satisfies $\lvert \theta' - P/d \rvert \ge \eta$.
The value of $\eta$ is not provided by Ridout's theorem.
The odd $q < 4\ell$ are finite in number, so take an odd $q \ge 4\ell$ with a cycle of length $\ell$, in the notation of Lemmas~\ref{lem:any-window} and~\ref{lem:three-approx}.
Then $\theta \in \Theta$.
Each $\theta_i$ either lies in $\Theta$ or is a power of $2$.

Consider the fraction $q/2^m$, which is in lowest terms.
Its left side in the general form equals $2^m \cdot \delta \cdot 2^{-m} = \delta$, since $\lvert 2^m \rvert_p = 1$ for every odd prime $p$.
Also $\max(q,\, 2^m) < q+1$, because $2^m = (q+\delta)/\theta < (q+1)/\theta < q+1$.
If $\delta < (q+1)^{-\varepsilon}$, then $\delta < \max(q,\, 2^m)^{-\varepsilon}$, so Ridout's inequality applies and $\delta/2^m \ge \eta$.
Combined with $2^m > q/2$ this yields $q < 2/\eta$.

Now assume $\delta \ge (q+1)^{-\varepsilon}$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:any-window} gives $\delta \le e^{\ell/q}/a \le e^{1/4}/a$, so $a < 2(q+1)^\varepsilon$.

Suppose first that $y \le q^{1-2\varepsilon}$, and choose $i$ with $x_i = y$.
If $\ell$ divides $ir$, then $\theta_i = 2^s$ for some integer $s \ge 1$.
This power is not equal to $F_i$.
Otherwise $x_i = a \cdot 2^{im - S_i + s}$; the right side is an odd positive integer only if the exponent of $2$ vanishes, which forces $x_i = a$, contradicting distinctness.
Let $e = \max(im - S_i,\, 0)$.
In lowest terms the denominator of $F_i$ divides $a \cdot 2^e$, so $\lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert \ge 1/(a \cdot 2^e)$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:three-approx} then gives $2^e \ge q/(2i\theta_i)$.
If $e = 0$, then $q \le 2i\theta_i$.
Here $i \le \ell-1$ and $\theta_i < 2^{\ell-1}$, so $q < (\ell-1)\, 2^\ell$.
If $e \ge 1$, the same lemma and $q \ge 4\ell$ give $\lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert \le 2(\ell-1)\theta_i/(4\ell) < \theta_i/2$, hence $F_i > \theta_i/2$.
Therefore
\[
y = F_i \cdot a \cdot 2^e > \frac{\theta_i}{2} \cdot a \cdot \frac{q}{2i\theta_i} = \frac{aq}{4i} \ge \frac{q}{4(\ell-1)}.
\]
Once $q > \bigl(4(\ell-1)\bigr)^{5\ell}$, the right side exceeds $q^{1-2\varepsilon}$, because $1/(2\varepsilon) = 5\ell$.
This contradicts $y \le q^{1-2\varepsilon}$.

If instead $\ell$ does not divide $ir$, then $\theta_i \in \Theta$.
Write $F_i = P/d$ in lowest terms.
The odd part of $d$ divides $a$, so $d \cdot \lvert d \rvert_2 \le a$.
Every factor $\lvert d \rvert_p$ is at most $1$ and $2 \in S$, so the left side of the general form is at most
\[
d^2 \cdot \lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert \cdot \lvert d \rvert_2 \le \frac{2i\theta_i\, d}{q}.
\]
As above, $\lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert < \theta_i/2$, so $F_i > \theta_i/2 > 1/2$ and $F_i < (3/2)\theta_i < 2^\ell$.
If $im \ge S_i$, the fraction before reducing is $y/(a \cdot 2^{im-S_i})$, whose denominator equals $y/F_i < 2y$, and the numerator is $y$.
If $im < S_i$, that denominator is $a$, so $d \le a \le y$, and the numerator is $F_i d < 2^\ell y$.
In both cases $d < 2y$ and $\max(\lvert P \rvert,\, d) < 2^\ell y$.
Also $2i\theta_i < (\ell-1)\, 2^\ell$, so the left side is less than $(\ell-1)\, 2^{\ell+1} y/q$.
The right side is greater than $(2^\ell y)^{-\varepsilon}$.
The comparison
\[
(\ell-1)\, 2^{\ell+1}\, y^{1+\varepsilon} < q \cdot 2^{-\ell\varepsilon}
\]
therefore implies Ridout's inequality.
It holds for every admissible $y$ once
\[
q^{\varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2} > (\ell-1)\, 2^{\ell + 1 + \ell\varepsilon},
\]
because $y^{1+\varepsilon} \le q^{(1-2\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)} = q^{1 - \varepsilon - 2\varepsilon^2}$.
The right side depends only on $\ell$.
For every larger $q$, Ridout's theorem gives $\lvert \theta_i - F_i \rvert \ge \eta$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:three-approx} gives $2i\theta_i/(aq) \ge \eta$, and $2i\theta_i < (\ell-1)\, 2^\ell$ with $a \ge 1$, so $q < (\ell-1)\, 2^\ell/\eta$.

Suppose instead that $y > q^{1-2\varepsilon}$.
The numerator $N = \ell aq + 1$ is congruent to $1$ modulo $\ell a$, so $\gcd(N,\, \ell a) = 1$.
Any common factor of $N$ and $\ell a \cdot 2^m$ is therefore a power of $2$.
Let $v = v_2(N)$ and $e = \max(m - v,\, 0)$.
In lowest terms, $W = P/d$ with $d = \ell a \cdot 2^e$ and $P \le N$.
The product formula gives $\prod_p \lvert \ell \rvert_p = 1/\ell$, and every prime factor of $\ell$ lies in $S$, so $\prod_{p \in S} \lvert \ell \rvert_p = 1/\ell$.
Since $\lvert a \rvert_p \le 1$,
\[
\prod_{p \in S} \lvert d \rvert_p \le \frac{2^{-e}}{\ell}.
\]
The left side of the general form is therefore at most
\[
d^2 \cdot \lvert \theta - W \rvert \cdot \frac{2^{-e}}{\ell} = \ell a^2\, 2^e \cdot \lvert \theta - W \rvert \le \ell a^2\, 2^m \cdot \lvert \theta - W \rvert.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:three-approx} and $2^m < q+1$ bound this by $2\ell a^2(1 + 1/q)(1/y + 2\ell/q)$.
Insert $1/y < q^{-1+2\varepsilon}$ and $a < 2(q+1)^\varepsilon$.
For $q \ge 4\ell$ one has $1 + 1/q < 2$, $1 + 2\ell\, q^{-2\varepsilon} \le 1 + 2\ell$, and $(q+1)^{2\varepsilon} < (2q)^{2\varepsilon}$, so the left side is less than
\[
16\ell(1+2\ell)\, 2^{2\varepsilon}\, q^{-1+4\varepsilon}.
\]
Since $P \le \ell aq + 1$ and $d \le \ell a \cdot 2^m < \ell a(q+1)$, the height satisfies $\max(\lvert P \rvert,\, d) < \ell a(q+1) + 1 < 4\ell(q+1)^{1+\varepsilon}$, so the right side is greater than $(4\ell)^{-\varepsilon}(q+1)^{-\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)} > (4\ell)^{-\varepsilon}(2q)^{-\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)}$.
The quotient of these two bounds is
\[
16\ell(1+2\ell)\, 2^{2\varepsilon}(4\ell)^\varepsilon\, 2^{\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)}\, q^{-1+5\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2}.
\]
The exponent equals $-1 + 5/(10\ell) + 1/(100\ell^2) \le -1 + 1/4 + 1/400 < -1/2$.
The quotient tends to $0$ as $q \to \infty$, so Ridout's inequality holds for all $q$ above a bound depending only on $\ell$.
Then $\lvert \theta - W \rvert \ge \eta$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:three-approx} and $1/y \le 1$ give $\lvert \theta - W \rvert \le 2(1+2\ell)/q$, hence $q \le (2+4\ell)/\eta$.

In every case $q$ is at most a number depending only on $\ell$ and $\eta$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Theorem~4 fails for $\ell = 1$.}
The Mersenne family $q = 2^e - 1$ gives the cycle $1 \to 1$ for every $e \ge 2$, and the same identity becomes $2^h = q + 1$.
The constant $\eta$ is not explicit, so neither is the bound on $q$.
An effective bound would require an effective inequality of the same shape for every denominator whose odd part is at most a constant times $q^\varepsilon$.

\section{A conjecture on all cycles}
\label{sec:all-cycles}

The complete list of known cycles is short.

A length-$1$ cycle is a fixed point $C(x_0) = x_0$, hence $x_0(2^h - q) = 1$.
The only positive odd solution is $x_0 = 1$ and $q = 2^h - 1$, so $1 \to 1$ when $q = 2^e - 1$ with $e \ge 2$, and there is no length-$1$ cycle with $x_0 > 1$.
For $q = 5$ and $h = 5$ there is $1 \to 3 \to 1$.
For $q = 5$ and $h = 7$ there are $13 \to 33 \to 83 \to 13$ and $17 \to 43 \to 27 \to 17$.
For $q = 181$ and $h = 15$ there are $27 \to 611 \to 27$ and $35 \to 99 \to 35$.
Crandall recorded the cycle $27 \to 611$ from the identity $2^{15} - 181^2 = 7$.

\begin{named}{Conjecture 5.}
These are all the cycles of $C$.
For every odd $q \ge 3$, every cycle is either $1 \to 1$ with $q = 2^e - 1$, or one of the five cycles at $q = 5$ and $q = 181$ listed above.
In particular, if $q \ge 7$ is neither $181$ nor of the form $2^e - 1$, then $C$ has no cycle, so every orbit diverges.
\end{named}

There is then no cycle through $1$ other than $1 \to 1$ for Mersenne $q$ and $1 \to 3 \to 1$ for $q = 5$, which is Santos's conjecture, and for $q = 3$ there is no cycle other than $1 \to 1$.
For those $q \ge 7$ that are neither $181$ nor Mersenne, the claim is stronger than Crandall's conjecture that some orbit avoids $1$: there is no cycle at all, and an infinite orbit of odd positive integers is unbounded.
The searches that found no others, in the ranges they cover, are in Appendix~\ref{app:computations}.

\topic{A heuristic.}
A cycle of length $\ell \ge 2$ with $h$ halvings has to satisfy several conditions at once.
As above, $q^\ell < 2^h < (q+1)^\ell$, so $\ell$ does not divide $h$ and $q = \lfloor 2^{h/\ell}\rfloor$ is a single odd integer at least $3$.
Write $D = 2^h - q^\ell$.
Starting from any element $x$ of the cycle, the halving counts are a composition of $h$ into $\ell$ positive parts, and the partial sums $S_j$ begin at $x$.
Unfolding the cycle relations gives
\[
x = \frac{N}{D}, \qquad N := q^{\ell-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1} q^{\ell-1-j}\, 2^{S_j}.
\]
Thus $D$ divides $N$, and $x$ is a positive odd integer.
Tracing from $x$ recovers those counts exactly, and the $\ell$ elements are odd and distinct.

If, for a random composition, $N$ is uniformly distributed modulo $D$, then $D$ divides $N$ with probability $1/D$.
Counting cycles up to rotation, the expected number at this pair $(h,\ell)$ is then about
\[
\frac{1}{\ell D}\binom{h-1}{\ell-1}.
\]
This is only a heuristic: $N$ is a sparse combination of powers of $2$ with coefficients powers of $q$, so the residue $N \bmod D$ can be constrained, and the remaining conditions only make a cycle rarer.

The estimate is large only when $D$ is small, that is when $q^\ell$ is extraordinarily close to a power of $2$.
The known cycles are exactly those coincidences:
\[
5^2 = 2^5 - 7, \qquad 5^3 = 2^7 - 3, \qquad 181^2 = 2^{15} - 7.
\]
The corresponding expectations are $4/(2\cdot 7) \approx 0.29$, $15/(3\cdot 3) = 5/3$, and $14/(2\cdot 7) = 1$, against one, two, and two cycles found.
The only consecutive perfect powers are $8$ and $9$, of the other sign, so $D \ge 3$ for $\ell \ge 2$, and $D = 3$ and $D = 7$ are as small as occur here.

The next-smallest values of $D$ do not produce cycles.
For $q = 11$ and $h = 7$ one has $11^2 = 2^7 - 7$, the same $D$ as for $q = 5$ and $q = 181$, and the estimate is $6/(2\cdot 7) \approx 0.43$; but $11 + 2^{k_1} \not\equiv 0 \pmod{7}$ for every positive $k_1 < 7$, so $D$ never divides $N$.
For $q = 45$ and $h = 11$, likewise $45^2 = 2^{11} - 23$ and $45 + 2^{k_1} \equiv 0 \pmod{23}$ forces $2^{k_1} \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$, hence $11 \mid k_1$, which is impossible for $0 < k_1 < 11$.
For $q = 3$ and $h = 5$, $3^3 = 2^5 - 5$ and the estimate is $0.4$; the length-$3$ search finds no cycle.
For $q = 3$ and $h = 8$, $3^5 = 2^8 - 13$ and the estimate is $0.54$; the length-$5$ search finds no cycle.
For $q = 19$ and $h = 17$, $19^4 = 2^{17} - 751$, the fourth-power near miss of Section~\ref{sec:open}, and the estimate is $0.19$; the length-$4$ search finds no cycle.

Summing the same estimate over every admissible pair with $2 \le \ell \le 20$ and $h \le 200$ gives a total of about $7$ expected cycles, of which about $3$ sit in the three known slots and the remaining $4$ are spread over pairs that the searches of Appendix~\ref{app:computations} have already emptied.
For large $h$ a typical $D$ is of size $\ell \delta\, 2^{h(1-1/\ell)}$, with $\delta$ the fractional part of $2^{h/\ell}$, and $\binom{h-1}{\ell-1}/D$ decays exponentially in $h$.
Length $1$ is different: $D = 2^h - q = 1$ holds for every Mersenne $q$, and $N = 1$, so the division succeeds for every $e$ and produces $1 \to 1$.

For a cycle at large $q$ there is a further obstruction.
A random odd input has $E[v_2(qn+1)] = 2$, while a cycle needs mean $h/\ell \approx \log_2 q$.
For $q = 5$ this is $2.32$, a mild excess, and three cycles exist.
For $q = 181$ it is $7.5$, which occurs only because $D = 7$.
For larger $q$ the same mean requires a still smaller relative gap, which is the Diophantine condition already used in Theorems~2 and~4.

\section{Other results}
\label{sec:other}

The following were found along the way.
They are not needed for Theorems~1, 2 and~4, Proposition~3, and Corollaries~12 to~14; full statements and proofs are in the appendices indicated, and Appendix~\ref{app:history} has notes on the literature and the history of the work.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One and two odd steps} (Appendix~\ref{app:one-two}).
The two-step case, only $q = 5$, follows from Beukers's theorem on $x^2 + D = 2^n$.
\item \textbf{The order of $2$} (Appendix~\ref{app:order}). $k_3 = d$ or $2d$, where $d$ is the order of $2$ modulo $q$; in particular $d < 3 \log_2 q$, and no Wieferich prime $q$ has a $3$-odd cycle through $1$, the same mechanism Franco and Pomerance use for Crandall's question of when orbits avoid $1$.
\item \textbf{Finiteness for three steps via Ridout alone} (Appendix~\ref{app:ridout}).
Lemmas~5 and~\ref{lem:window} bound the window by about $q^{-1/2}$, and Ridout's theorem then gives finiteness through~$1$.
This is the case $\ell = 3$ of Proposition~3.
Theorem~4 is the same range of lengths, for cycles that need not visit~$1$, and uses the general form of Ridout's theorem.
\item \textbf{Elementary special cases} (Appendix~\ref{app:elementary}). $k_1 = k_2 = 1$ is impossible via the integral points of $Y^2 = X^3 - 4X^2 + 12X + 16$ ($q = 41$ is a near miss), and $k_1 = 1$, $k_2 = 2$ via the factorization $(q+2)(q^2 - q + 4)$.
\item \textbf{Bounds on $k_1$} (Appendix~\ref{app:k1}).
Bauer and Bennett's inequalities give $q^{1/3}/2 < 2^{k_1} < 2.01(q+1)^{2/5}$, so each $k_1$ allows only $q$ in an explicit finite range, and $k_1 = 1$ is impossible.
\item \textbf{The cases of Lemma~4} (Appendix~\ref{app:cases}).
The cases $t > k_1$ and $t = k_1$ are impossible, and the case $t < k_1$ is tightly constrained.
\item \textbf{Small elements and an earlier bound on $y$} (Appendix~\ref{app:small}).
In fact $x \ge 5$ and $y \ge 7$.
Before Lemma~6, the best result was $y \ge x$ or $y > (3q)^{1/3}$; this is the origin of the threshold $q \ge 71$ in earlier versions of the window lemma.
\item \textbf{Work on four odd steps} (Appendix~\ref{app:four}). The analogues of Lemmas~1 and~5: $k_4 \ge k_1 + 3$, $k_4 \ge 3k_1 - 1$, and $x > \sqrt{q/2}$. A Ridout plan through~$1$ needed the same lower bounds for $y$ and $z$; two configurations were left open, and Proposition~3 makes that plan unnecessary. Theorem~4 gives ineffective finiteness for an arbitrary cycle of length~$4$.
\item \textbf{Computations} (Appendix~\ref{app:computations}).
Searches over $h$ for cycles through $1$ with $\ell = 3$ to $15$, a search for an arbitrary cycle with $\ell = 2$ to $9$, a search at fixed $q = 5$ and at each odd $q$ from $7$ through $25$, verification of the Bauer--Bennett inputs, and the digit scan ruling out $q < 2^{1{,}071{,}999{,}953}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Open problems}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Four steps with $h$ odd.}
The window and the near misses for $x_1$ and $x_3$ involve $2^{1/4}$ and $2^{3/4}$.
Theorem~2 would finish the case given an effective exponent below $\varphi$ for these numbers with power-of-$2$ denominators.
The Bauer--Bennett construction needs a fourth power exceptionally close to a power of $2$; among odd $a \le 3 \times 10^6$ the best is $19^4 = 2^{17} - 751$, of quality $\log_2(2^{17})/\log_2 751 \approx 1.78$, against $4.4$ for the cube case ($5^3 + 3 = 2^7$) and $5.3$ for $\sqrt{2}$ ($181^2 + 7 = 2^{15}$).
A different construction is likely needed.
\item \textbf{Effective results for larger $\ell$.}
Theorem~2 turns any such exponent for the roots $2^{r/\ell}$ into a complete answer for that $\ell$.
Santos's conjecture in full would need this for every $\ell$, or a bound uniform in $\ell$.
\item \textbf{All cycles.}
Conjecture~5 is open.
Theorem~4 gives, for each $\ell \ge 2$, only finitely many $q$ with a cycle of length $\ell$, with no bound and no classification.
The heuristic of Section~\ref{sec:all-cycles} is not a proof, and the searches leave infinitely many pairs $(\ell, h)$ unchecked.
\item \textbf{Novelty.}
Franco's 1990 thesis on Diophantine approximation and the $qx+1$ problem could not be consulted and should be checked for overlap, including with Theorem~4.
\end{enumerate}

\section{One and two odd steps}
\label{app:one-two}

\topic{One odd step.}
A length-$1$ cycle is $C(x_0) = x_0$, so $x_0(2^h - q) = 1$.
The only positive odd solution is $x_0 = 1$ and $q = 2^h - 1$.
In particular the cycle through $1$ is $1 \to 1$ precisely when $q = 2^e - 1$ (Santos, Proposition~15).

\begin{named}{Proposition A.1 (two odd steps).}
The only odd $q$ with $1$ on a cycle of $C$ of length $2$ is $q = 5$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The cycle gives $q(q+1) = 2^{k_1}(2^{k_2} - 1)$.
Multiplying by $4$ and adding $1$,
\[
(2q+1)^2 + \bigl(2^{k_1+2} - 1\bigr) = 2^{k_1+k_2+2}.
\]
This is $X^2 + D = 2^n$ with $X = 2q+1$ and $D = 2^{k_1+2} - 1$.
By a theorem of Beukers (Theorem~1.8 in Bauer and Bennett 2002), for $D \ne 7$, $23$ and $D$ not of the form $2^k - 1$ there is at most one solution, while for $D = 2^k - 1$ with $k \ge 4$ there are exactly two, $X = 1$ and $X = 2^{k-1} - 1$.
These give $q = 0$ and $q = 2^{k_1} - 1$, the one-step cycle traversed twice, so $k_1 \ge 2$ gives nothing new.
For $k_1 = 1$, $D = 7$ is the Ramanujan--Nagell equation with $X \in \{1, 3, 5, 11, 181\}$, and only $X = 11$ gives an odd $q$ other than $1$: $q = 5$, with $1 \to 3 \to 1$.
\end{proof}

As a check, the argument of Lemma~B.2 below gives $2^{k_2} = qx + 1 < q^2 < 2^{2d}$ with $d$ the order of $2$ modulo $q$, so $k_2 = d$, and the cycle condition reads $q(q+1) = 2^{k_1}(2^d - 1)$; for $q = 5$, $d = 4$ and $5 \cdot 6 = 2 \cdot 15$.

\section{Earlier results on three odd steps}
\label{sec:earlier}

These results were found before Theorem~1 and are not needed for it.
Notation is as in Sections~\ref{sec:structure} to~\ref{sec:proof}.
Bauer and Bennett's headline inequality, $|x^3 - 2^n| \ge x^{4/3}$ unless $x^3 = 2^n$ or $(x, n) = (5, 7)$, and their Corollary~4, $|x^3 - 27 \cdot 2^n| \ge 3^{5/3} x^{4/3}$ unless $x^3 = 27 \cdot 2^n$ or $x \in \{4, 5, 8, 15, 19, 38, 121\}$, are used throughout; both were also checked numerically for $n < 200$ and $n < 70$ respectively.

\subsection{Factorization and the order of $2$}
\label{app:order}

\begin{named}{Lemma B.1 (factorization).}
With $q = 2^{k_1} x - 1$,
\[
\bigl(2^{k_1} x - 1\bigr) \bigl(2^{k_1} x^2 - x + 1\bigr) = 2^{k_2} \bigl(2^{k_3} - 1\bigr).
\]
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The factors are $q$ and $qx + 1$, and $q(qx + 1) = q \cdot 2^{k_2} y = 2^{k_2}(2^{k_3} - 1)$.
\end{proof}

Completing the square in $qx + 1 = 2^{k_2} y$ gives $(2q+1)^2 + (2^{k_1+2} - 1) = 2^{k_1+k_2+2} \cdot y$, so the three-step case is the equation of Proposition~A.1 with an odd cofactor $y$, tied to $q$ by $qy + 1 = 2^{k_3}$.

\begin{named}{Lemma B.2 (order of $2$).}
$k_3 = d$ or $k_3 = 2d$, where $d$ is the order of $2$ modulo $q$.
In particular $d < 3 \log_2 q$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
$2^{k_3} = qy + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$, so $d$ divides $k_3$.
Using $2^{k_1} \ge 2$ and $2^{k_2} \ge 2$, $y \le (q^2 + q + 2)/4$, so $2^{k_3} \le q(q^2 + q + 2)/4 + 1 < q^3$.
Also $2^d \ge q + 1 > q$, so $q^3 < 2^{3d}$, and hence $k_3 < 3d$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Corollary B.3 (Wieferich primes).}
Let $F = (2^d - 1)/q$ and $k_3 = md$.
Then $mF \equiv 2^{-k_2} \pmod{q}$, so $\gcd(F, q) = 1$.
In particular no Wieferich prime $q$ ($q^2 \mid 2^{q-1} - 1$) has a $3$-odd cycle through $1$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Since $2^d \equiv 1 \pmod{q}$, $y = (2^{md} - 1)/q = F\bigl(1 + 2^d + \cdots + 2^{(m-1)d}\bigr) \equiv mF \pmod{q}$.
Reducing $2^{k_2} y = qx + 1 \bmod q$ gives $2^{k_2} \cdot mF \equiv 1$.
For a Wieferich prime, the lifting-the-exponent lemma shows that $q^2$ divides $2^d - 1$, since $q^2$ divides $2^{q-1} - 1$ and $q$ does not divide $(q-1)/d$; so $q \mid F$.
\end{proof}

This is the mechanism Franco and Pomerance use for Crandall's question of when orbits avoid $1$.
Together with Lemma~\ref{lem:cube-window}, Lemma~B.2 determines everything from $q$: $k_1 = v_2(q+1)$, $h$ is the unique exponent with $q^3 < 2^h < (q+1)^3$, $k_3 \in \{d, 2d\}$ and $k_2 = h - k_1 - k_3$.

\subsection{Finiteness via Ridout's theorem}
\label{app:ridout}

\begin{named}{Proposition B.4.}
Only finitely many odd $q$ have a $3$-odd cycle through $1$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:window} and~5, $\delta - 1/3 < 2^{k_1+1}/(3q)$ with $2^{k_1} = (q+1)/x < (q+1)\sqrt{2/q}$, so $\delta - 1/3 < 0.96\, q^{-1/2}$ for $q \ge 71$.
With $h = 3m + r$, $\beta = 2^{r/3}$, $P = 3q + 1$ and $Q = 3 \cdot 2^m$ this gives $0 < \beta - P/Q < 2.9\, q^{-1/2}/Q$, and since $Q < 2.39(q+1)$ we get $|\beta - P/Q| < 4.6\, Q^{-3/2} < Q^{-5/4}$ for $Q > 450$.
The reduced fraction still has a denominator built from $2$ and $3$, so Ridout's theorem with $S = \{2, 3\}$ and $\varepsilon = 1/4$ allows only finitely many such fractions; infinitely many $q$ would give infinitely many distinct ones, since the errors tend to $0$ and $\beta$ is irrational.
\end{proof}

This is the case $\ell = 3$ of Proposition~3, and Theorem~1 supersedes it.
The exponent $3/2$ comes from the lower bounds $x > \sqrt{q/2}$ and $y > x$; with only $x, y \ge 3$ the error is of order $1/Q$ and Ridout's theorem gives nothing.
The result is ineffective, which is why Theorem~1 needs Theorem~(Bauer--Bennett) instead.

\subsection{Elementary special cases}
\label{app:elementary}

\begin{named}{Proposition B.5 ($k_1 = k_2 = 1$).}
No $3$-odd cycle through $1$ has $k_1 = k_2 = 1$.
Hence $q \not\equiv 1 \pmod{8}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~2 gives $2^h = q^3 + q^2 + 2q + 4 = (q+1)(q^2 + 2) + 2$.
With $q + 1 = 2x$ this becomes
\[
4x^3 - 4x^2 + 3x + 1 = 2^n, \qquad n = h - 1,
\]
and $q^2 + 2 = 4x^2 - 4x + 3$ divides $2^n - 1$.
Since $q$ is odd, $q^2 + 2 \equiv 3 \pmod{8}$.
Every prime $p$ dividing $q^2 + 2$ has $-2$ as a square mod $p$, so $p \equiv 1$ or $3 \pmod{8}$, and at least one is $\equiv 3 \pmod{8}$.
For that $p$, $2$ is not a square mod $p$, so the order of $2$ modulo $p$ is even and divides $n$.
So $n$ is even, and $(X, Y) = (4x,\ 4 \cdot 2^{n/2})$ is an integral point on
\[
E \colon Y^2 = X^3 - 4X^2 + 12X + 16.
\]
Using Sage (passagemath) with proofs enabled, $E$ has rank $2$ and trivial torsion, generated by $(0, 4)$ and $(1, 5)$ with saturation index $1$, and its integral points are $X \in \{0, 1, 4, 9, 84\}$, computed by the elliptic-logarithm method. $X = 4$ gives $q = 1$; $X = 84$ gives $x = 21$ but $Y = 752 = 4 \cdot 188$, and $188$ is not a power of $2$; the others do not have the form $4x$ with $x$ odd and at least $3$.
For the congruence: if $q \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, then $k_1 = 1$ and $x \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, so $t > k_1$ in Lemma~4, and its proof forces $k_2 = k_1 = 1$.
\end{proof}

The point $X = 84$ is a near miss: for $q = 41$, $q^3 + q^2 + 2q + 4$ is twice a perfect square, and the cycle fails only at the last step, since $41 \cdot 431 + 1 = 17{,}672 = 8 \cdot 47^2$.

\begin{named}{Proposition B.6 ($k_1 = 1$, $k_2 = 2$).}
No $3$-odd cycle through $1$ has $k_1 = 1$ and $k_2 = 2$.
Hence $q \not\equiv 13 \pmod{16}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~2 gives $2^h = q^3 + q^2 + 2q + 8 = (q+2)(q^2 - q + 4)$, and $q+2$ is an odd number at least $5$.
For $k_1 = 1$, $k_2 = 2$ holds exactly when $x \equiv 7 \pmod{8}$, that is $q \equiv 13 \pmod{16}$.
\end{proof}

Both are superseded by Corollary~B.8, which rules out $k_1 = 1$ altogether.

\subsection{Effective bounds on $k_1$}
\label{app:k1}

\begin{named}{Proposition B.7.}
In any $3$-odd cycle through $1$, $2^{k_1} > q^{1/3}/2$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Apply Bauer and Bennett's Corollary~4 with $x = 3q + 1$ and $n = h$.
The exceptional values of $x$ give $q \in \{1, 6, 40\}$, which are excluded, and $N > 0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:window}, so $N \ge 3^{5/3}(3q+1)^{4/3} > 27\, q^{4/3}$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:window}, $N < 54 \cdot 2^{k_1} q$.
Comparing, $54 \cdot 2^{k_1} q > 27\, q^{4/3}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Corollary B.8.}
(a) $k_1 = 1$ is impossible: it would force $q < 64$, and the direct search excludes every $q < 2{,}000{,}000$; so every possible $q$ is $\equiv 3 \pmod{4}$.
(b) Together with Lemma~5, $2^{2k_1-1} - 1 < q < 2^{3k_1+3}$, so each $k_1$ allows only an explicit finite range of $q$.
(c) Since the digit scan (Appendix~\ref{app:computations}) excludes $q < 2^{1{,}071{,}999{,}953}$, any remaining $q$ would need $k_1 \ge 357{,}333{,}317$, so $q \equiv -1 \pmod{2^{357{,}333{,}317}}$.
\end{named}

Theorem~1 rules out these $q$ altogether.

\begin{named}{Proposition B.9 (upper bound on $k_1$).}
$2^{k_1} \le 3.2\, x^{2/3}$, and hence $2^{k_1} < 2.01(q+1)^{2/5}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:near-x}, $x^3 - 2^f = E/2^{3k_1} \ne 0$ with $|E| \le 3.2 \cdot 2^{2k_1} x^2$, so Bauer and Bennett's headline inequality gives $2^{3k_1} x^{4/3} \le 3.2 \cdot 2^{2k_1} x^2$.
The exception $x = 5$ forces $q \le 39$ by Lemma~5.
\end{proof}

Together with Proposition~B.7, $q^{1/3}/2 < 2^{k_1} < 2.01(q+1)^{2/5}$.

\subsection{The three cases of Lemma 4}
\label{app:cases}

\begin{named}{Proposition B.10 ($t > k_1$ is impossible).}
No $3$-odd cycle through $1$ has $t > k_1$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~4 and its proof give $t = 2k_1$ and $k_2 = k_1$, so $x = 1 + 2^{2k_1} s$ with $s$ odd.
Proposition~B.7 gives $q < 8 \cdot 2^{3k_1}$, so $x < 8 \cdot 2^{2k_1}$ and $s \in \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$.
Then $q = s \cdot 2^{3k_1} + 2^{k_1} - 1$, and the cycle equation reads $2^h = q^3 + q^2 + 2^{k_1} q + 2^{2k_1}$.
Dividing by $2^{9k_1}$,
\[
s^3 < \frac{2^h}{2^{9k_1}} < \left(s + \frac{1}{2^{2k_1}}\right)^3 \left(1 + \frac{2}{q}\right),
\]
and for $2^{k_1} \ge 8$ the right-hand side is below the next power of $2$ after $s^3$ ($2$, $32$, $128$, $512$), so $2^h/2^{9k_1}$ lies strictly between consecutive powers of $2$.
For $2^{k_1} \le 4$, $q \le 451$, which the direct search covers.
\end{proof}
(Also checked in exact arithmetic for $k_1 < 400$.)

\begin{named}{Proposition B.11 ($t = k_1$ is impossible).}
No $3$-odd cycle through $1$ has $t = k_1$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Here $x = 1 + 2^{k_1} s$ with $s$ odd, so $q = 2^{2k_1} s + 2^{k_1} - 1$.
Let $g = h - 6k_1$.
Expanding the cycle equation,
\[
0 < 2^g - s^3 = \frac{R}{2^{6k_1}} \le \frac{6s^2}{2^{k_1}},
\qquad
R = 3 \cdot 2^{4k_1} s^2(2^{k_1}-1) + 3 \cdot 2^{2k_1} s(2^{k_1}-1)^2 + (2^{k_1}-1)^3 + q^2 + 2^{k_1} q + 2^{k_1 + k_2},
\]
using $q < 2 \cdot 2^{2k_1} s$ and $2^{k_2} < q$ ($2^{k_1} \ge 4$). Bauer and Bennett's headline inequality gives $2^g - s^3 \ge s^{4/3}$, so $2^{k_1} \le 6\, s^{2/3}$, while Proposition~B.7 gives $8 \cdot 2^{3k_1} > q > 2^{2k_1} s$, so $2^{k_1} > s/8$.
Hence $s < 48^3$ and $2^{k_1} \le 13{,}824$, so $q < 2 \cdot 2^{2k_1} s < 5 \times 10^{13}$, which the computations cover.
The exception $s = 5$ gives $q < 4 \times 10^6$ through Proposition~B.9.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Proposition B.12 (a constraint when $t < k_1$).}
Suppose $t < k_1$, so that $k_2 = t$, and write $x = 2^{k_1} u - (2^{k_2}-1)$ with $u$ odd.
Then $2^{k_1}/2^{k_2} \le 3u^{2/3}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Put $\delta' = 2^{k_1 + k_2} - 2^{k_1} + 1$, so $q = 2^{2k_1} u - \delta'$, and $g = h - 6k_1$.
Expanding,
\[
0 < u^3 - 2^g
= \frac{3 \cdot 2^{4k_1} u^2 \delta' - 3 \cdot 2^{2k_1} u \delta'^2 + \delta'^3 - q^2 - 2^{k_1} q - 2^{k_1 + k_2}}{2^{6k_1}}
\le \frac{3u^2 \cdot 2^{k_2}}{2^{k_1}},
\]
since the terms after the first are negative in total and $\delta' < 2^{k_1 + k_2}$.
Bauer and Bennett's headline inequality gives $u^3 - 2^g \ge u^{4/3}$.
The exceptions are $u = 1$, where $x \le 2^{k_1}$ and Proposition~B.9 forces $2^{k_1} \le 32$, and $u = 5$, where it forces $q < 4 \times 10^6$.
\end{proof}

The positivity and upper bounds in Propositions~B.11 and~B.12 were also checked in exact arithmetic on hundreds of thousands of random parameter choices.
Propositions~B.10 and~B.11 leave only the case $t < k_1$; Theorem~1 makes the case analysis unnecessary.

\subsection{Small elements and an earlier bound on $y$}
\label{app:small}

\begin{named}{Remark (small elements).}
The elements $x$ and $y$ are odd and different from $1$, so $x, y \ge 3$, and Corollary~7 gives $y \ge 5$.
If $x = 3$, Lemma~5 gives $2^{k_1} \le 2(x-1) = 4$, so $q = 3 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 1 \in \{5, 11\}$, and neither has a $3$-odd cycle through $1$.
Hence $x \ge 5$ and $y \ge 7$.
None of the proofs in the main text need more than $x \ge 3$ and $y > x$.
\end{named}

Before Lemma~6 was found, the case $y < x$ could not be excluded, and the following weaker bound was used instead.
Corollary~7 shows that $y < x$ never happens, so the second alternative below is vacuous.

\begin{named}{Proposition B.13.}
In a $3$-odd cycle through $1$, either $y \ge x$ or
\[
y > \left( \frac{3q^2}{q+1} \right)^{1/3} \approx (3q)^{1/3}.
\]
Moreover, in the second case $v_2(y-1) = k_1$ exactly.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Multiplying $2^{k_2} y = qx + 1$ by $2^{k_1} y^2$ and using $qy = 2^{k_3} - 1$ and $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$ gives the exact identity
\[
2^{2k_3} + (y-2)\, 2^{k_3} + C = 2^{k_1+k_2} y^3,
\qquad
C = 2^{k_1} y^2 - y + 1.
\]
If $2^{k_3}$ divides $C$, then $C \ge 2^{k_3} = qy + 1$, which simplifies to $2^{k_1} y \ge q + 1$, that is $y \ge x$.
Otherwise the first two terms are divisible by a higher power of $2$ than $C$, so $v_2(C) = k_1 + k_2$ and $2^{k_1+k_2} \le C < 2^{k_1} y^2$.
Since the left side exceeds $2^{2k_3} > q^2 y^2$, this gives $q^2 y^2 < 2^{k_1+k_2} y^3 < 2^{k_1} y^5$, so $q^2 < 2^{k_1} y^3 \le (q+1) y^3 / 3$.
For the last claim, $y \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$ by Lemma~1; if $v_2(y-1) > k_1$, then $v_2(C) = k_1$, forcing $k_2 = 0$, which is impossible.
\end{proof}

For $q \ge 71$ the cube root is the smaller of the two bounds, so this gave $y > 1.436\, q^{1/3}$ unconditionally, and for $q > 2{,}000{,}000$ it gave $y \ge 183$.
The identity was also checked by computer algebra: substituting the cycle relations into both sides and expanding gives the same expression.

\section{Work on four odd steps}
\label{app:four}

\topic{Reduction to lower bounds.}
Before Proposition~3, finiteness for $\ell$ odd steps through~$1$ was reduced to a power of $q$ for every element other than $1$.

\begin{named}{Proposition C.1 (reduction to lower bounds).}
Fix $\ell$.
If some $\theta > 0$ and $c > 0$ make every element other than $1$ of every $\ell$-odd cycle through $1$ at least $c \cdot q^\theta$, then only finitely many $q$ have such a cycle.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
As for Proposition~B.4, with $h = \ell m + r$, $\beta = 2^{r/\ell}$, $P = \ell q + 1$ and $Q = \ell \cdot 2^m$: Lemma~\ref{lem:power-window} gives $|\beta - P/Q| \ll Q^{-1-\theta}$, and Ridout's theorem applies with $S = \{2\} \cup \{\text{primes dividing }\ell\}$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~3 makes the reduction unnecessary: Lemma~\ref{lem:near-misses} already supplies those lower bounds.

\topic{The first and last steps.}
Write a $4$-odd cycle through $1$ as $1 \to x \to y \to z \to 1$, with
\[
q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x, \qquad qx + 1 = 2^{k_2} y, \qquad qy + 1 = 2^{k_3} z, \qquad qz + 1 = 2^{k_4}.
\]
The next three lemmas are the four-step forms of Lemma~1 and Lemma~5, together with the gain from a larger $x$ in the last step. They constrain $k_4$ and $x$, not $y$ or $z$.

\begin{named}{Lemma C.2.}
$k_4 \ge k_1 + 3$, and $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The odd elements other than $1$ are at least $3$, so $x \ge 3$ and $z \ge 3$.
The first step is $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$, and therefore $q + 1 \ge 3 \cdot 2^{k_1}$, that is $q \ge 3 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 1$.
The last step is $2^{k_4} = qz + 1$, so
\[
2^{k_4} \ge (3 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 1) \cdot 3 + 1 = 9 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 2.
\]
Since $2^{k_1} \ge 2$, we have $9 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 2 \ge 8 \cdot 2^{k_1} = 2^{k_1 + 3}$, and hence $k_4 \ge k_1 + 3$.
From $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$ we also have $q \equiv -1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
Thus $qz + 1 \equiv 1 - z \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
But $qz + 1 = 2^{k_4}$ is divisible by $2^{k_1}$, because $k_4 \ge k_1$, so $1 - z \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$, that is $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma C.3.}
$2^{k_1} \le 2(x-1)$ and $x > \sqrt{q/2}$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~C.2, $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$, so $qy + 1 = 2^{k_3} z$ gives $y \equiv 1 - 2^{k_3} \pmod{2^{k_1}}$.
Let $t = v_2(x-1)$ and $s = (x-1)/2^t$.
If $t \ge k_1$, then $2^{k_1} \le x - 1$.
Otherwise $k_2 = t$ and $y = 2^{k_1-t}x^2 - s \equiv 2^{k_1-t} - s \pmod{2^{k_1}}$, since $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{2^t}$.
So $M = 2^{k_1-t} + 2^{k_3} - (s+1)$ is a multiple of $2^{k_1}$.
If $M = 0$, then $2^{k_1-t} < s + 1$, so $2^{k_1} < 2^t(s+1) \le 2(x-1)$.
If $M \le -2^{k_1}$, then $s + 1 > 2^{k_1}$, so $2^{k_1} \le x$.
If $M \ge 2^{k_1}$, then $2^{k_3} > 2^{k_1-1}$, so $k_3 \ge k_1$ and $2^{k_1-t} \equiv s + 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$, giving $2^{k_1-t} = s + 1$ or $s + 1 \ge 2^{k_1}$; either way $2^{k_1} \le 2(x-1)$.
The first step is $q + 1 = 2^{k_1} x$, so
\[
q + 1 \le 2x(x-1) = 2x^2 - 2x.
\]
Hence $q < 2x^2$, that is $x > \sqrt{q/2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{named}{Lemma C.4.}
$k_4 \ge 2k_1 + 1$ and $k_4 \ge 3k_1 - 1$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~C.3, $x \ge 2^{k_1-1} + 1$.
Since $z \ge 3$, the last step gives $2^{k_4} = qz + 1 \ge 3q + 1$.
The first step is $q = 2^{k_1} x - 1$, so $3q + 1 = 3 \cdot 2^{k_1} x - 2$, and therefore
\[
2^{k_4} \ge 3 \cdot 2^{2k_1-1} + 3 \cdot 2^{k_1} - 2 > 2^{2k_1},
\]
so $k_4 \ge 2k_1 + 1$.

By Lemma~C.2, $z \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{k_1}}$ and $z \ne 1$, so $z \ge 1 + 2^{k_1}$.
Then $2^{k_4} = qz + 1 \ge \bigl(2^{k_1} x - 1\bigr)\bigl(2^{k_1} + 1\bigr) + 1 = 2^{k_1}\bigl(x(2^{k_1}+1) - 1\bigr)$.
Substituting $x \ge 2^{k_1-1} + 1$ yields
\[
x(2^{k_1}+1) - 1 \ge \bigl(2^{k_1-1} + 1\bigr)\bigl(2^{k_1} + 1\bigr) - 1 = 2^{2k_1-1} + 2^{k_1-1} + 2^{k_1},
\]
hence
\[
2^{k_4} \ge 2^{3k_1-1} + 2^{2k_1-1} + 2^{2k_1} = 2^{3k_1-1} + 3 \cdot 2^{2k_1-1} > 2^{3k_1-1},
\]
so $k_4 \ge 3k_1 - 1$.
\end{proof}

\topic{The middle elements.}

\begin{named}{Lemma C.5.}
If $k_2 \le k_3$, then either $y > q$, or $y > x$ and $y^2 > qx$.
In particular $y \gg q^{3/4}$.
If $k_3 \le k_4$, then either $z > q$, or $z > y$ and $z^2 > qy$.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~6 to $x \to y \to z$, so $J = k_2$ and $K = k_3$.
If $k_2 \le k_3$ and $y < x$, then $y > q$; if $y > x$, then $y^2 > qx$.
In the first case $y \gg q^{3/4}$.
In the second, Lemma~C.3 gives $x > \sqrt{q/2}$, so $y^2 > q^{3/2}/\sqrt{2}$ and again $y \gg q^{3/4}$.
Apply Lemma~6 to $y \to z \to 1$, so $J = k_3$ and $K = k_4$.
If $k_3 \le k_4$ and $z < y$, then $z > q$; if $z > y$, then $z^2 > qy$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~C.5 bounds $y$ only when $k_2 \le k_3$, and $z$ only when $k_3 \le k_4$.
It leaves open $k_3 < k_2$ with $y < x$, where $y$ could be small, and $k_4 < k_3$, where $z$ could be small.
Those two lower bounds are the missing inputs for Proposition~C.1 in length $4$.
Theorem~4 does not need them.

\item \textbf{The lower bound} $L(r, n_1) \le \log \Pi$ was computed exactly for every $497 \le n_1 < 10^6$ and both $r$.
It reproduces the paper's values $\exp(L(2, 3319)/3319) = 1.897735\ldots$ and $\exp(L(1, 10000)/10000) = 2.006523\ldots$.
It exceeds $\log 1.8$ except for $43$ pairs $(n_1, r)$, all with $n_1 \le 830$; for these $\Pi$ was computed exactly for both $\delta$, with minimum $\Pi^{1/n_1} = 1.80767$ at $n_1 = 497$.
For $1000 < n_1 < 10^6$ the minimum of $\exp(L/n_1)$ is $1.8422$.
\item \textbf{The analytic estimates} for $n_1 \ge 10^6$ were recomputed: $0.59197\, n_1$ for $10^6 \le n_1 \le 2 \times 10^9$ and $0.59035\, n_1$ for $n_1 > 2 \times 10^9$, against the required $\log 1.8 \cdot n_1 = 0.58779\, n_1$.
The explicit prime bound they rely on (Bennett, Martin, O'Bryant and Rechnitzer) was spot-checked for $x \le 2 \times 10^8$, with maximum ratio $1.79628$ against the stated $1.798158$, and its two ranges meet at $x = 641{,}239{,}200.2$ as claimed.
\end{itemize}
The remaining external inputs are that prime bound for $x > 2 \times 10^8$, Lemma~4 of Beukers (one choice of $\delta$ gives $\Lambda \ne 0$) and Lemma~4.5 of Chudnovsky, used inside their Lemma~9.

\topic{Checks of our lemmas.}
The identities in Lemmas~2, 4, 6, 8 and~9 were checked by computer algebra (substituting the cycle relations and expanding both sides) or on random orbits, Lemma~4 numerically for $k_1 \le 7$ and all odd $x < 20{,}000$, and the elimination in step~3 of the proof of Theorem~1 for every $f$ from $33{,}000$ to $400{,}000$.

\begin{named}{Proposition D.1 (digit test).}
Let $q \ge 17$, write $h = 3m + r$ with $r \in \{1, 2\}$, and let $\beta = 2^{r/3}$.
Then $q = \lfloor 2^m \beta \rfloor$, and binary digits $m+1$ through $m + \lfloor m/2 \rfloor - 2$ of $\beta$ alternate $0, 1, 0, 1, \ldots$.
In particular, for $m \ge 100$ the $48$ digits $m+1$ through $m+48$ alternate.
\end{named}

\begin{proof}
The fractional part of $2^m \beta$ is $\delta$, made of $\beta$'s digits from position $m+1$ on.
Lemma~\ref{lem:window} gives $\delta - 1/3 < 2^{k_1+1}/(3q)$ (this uses $y > x$ from Corollary~7, through $2^{k_1+k_2} \le 2^{k_1} q$), and Lemma~5 gives $2^{k_1} = (q+1)/x < (q+1)\sqrt{2/q}$, so
\[
0 < \delta - \frac{1}{3}
< \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\left(1 + \frac{1}{q}\right) q^{-1/2}
\le q^{-1/2}
\le 2^{-\lfloor m/2 \rfloor},
\]
where the middle step uses $q \ge 17$ and the last uses $q \ge 2^m$.
As $1/3 = 0.010101\ldots{}_2$, an interval of width $2^{-\lfloor m/2 \rfloor}$ just above $1/3$ fixes all but the last two of $\delta$'s first $\lfloor m/2 \rfloor$ digits.
For $m \ge 100$ that is at least $48$ digits.
\end{proof}

Earlier versions stated this for $q \ge 71$, a threshold inherited from Proposition~B.13: there the guaranteed bound on $y$ was the smaller of $\sqrt{q/2}$ and $\bigl(3q^2/(q+1)\bigr)^{1/3}$, and the cube root is the smaller one exactly when $q^2 - 70q + 1 > 0$.
With Corollary~7 that crossover no longer matters.
Testing only the fixed $48$-digit prefix lets the scan cover every $m$ up to $N - 48$, where $N$ is the number of digits computed.

\topic{The run.}
$N = 1{,}072{,}000{,}000$ digits of each $\beta$ were computed with MPFR and certified twice: by MPFR's correct rounding (the $192$ guard digits are neither all $0$ nor all $1$, and the leading $61$ digits match an exact integer cube root), and independently by the exact test $I^3 < 2^{3N+r} < (I+1)^3$ on bit lengths, where $I = \lfloor 2^N \beta \rfloor$.
MPFR returned wrong values above about $2^{30}$ digits of precision, which these checks caught, so $N$ was kept just below that.
After XOR with \texttt{0x55}, an alternating run of $31$ or more digits shows up as three equal constant bytes, so the scan is linear.
The longest alternating run anywhere was $32$ digits, and no position has a $48$-digit alternating prefix.
With the search over $h$ for $m < 100$, this rules out every $q < 2^{1{,}071{,}999{,}953}$.
Computing and scanning took about $1.5$ minutes per $\beta$, and the cubing certificate about $1.5$ minutes more.

\section{Notes on the literature and history}
\label{app:history}

\topic{Literature.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Santos's preprint proves the one- and two-step cases (Propositions~15 and~16) and conjectures that $1$ is periodic only for $q = 5$ and $q = 2^e - 1$ (Conjecture~3).
His Remark~5 describes three or more odd steps as far harder and likely out of reach; Theorem~1 settles three steps.
The same preprint claims a proof of the Collatz conjecture, which is not accepted; only its elementary propositions are relied on here.
\item Crandall (1978) and Franco and Pomerance (1995) study when orbits of $qx+1$ maps avoid $1$; Corollary~B.3 matches their mechanism.
\item Franco's 1990 thesis uses Diophantine approximation for the $qx+1$ problem.
Only its title was available, and it is the most important item to check for overlap.
\item Steiner (1977, 1981) rules out one-block ``circuits'' for $q = 3$, $5$, $7$ with Baker's method, a related use of Diophantine approximation for cycles.
\item The deep inputs are Bauer and Bennett (2002, 2018) on restricted irrationality measures, Beukers on $X^2 + D = 2^n$, and Ridout (1958) on approximation with $S$-unit denominators and with denominators divisible by primes outside a fixed finite set.
\end{itemize}

\topic{History of the results.}
The work began with the window (Lemma~\ref{lem:window}), which pins down $q$ from $h$ and makes a search over $h$ far faster than a search over $q$.
Lower bounds on $x$ and $y$ (Lemmas~4 to~6 and Corollary~7, preceded by the weaker Proposition~B.13, which allowed $y < x$ with $y > (3q)^{1/3}$) then gave finiteness through Ridout's theorem (Proposition~B.4), but no bound.
Residue-class arguments settled special cases (Propositions~B.5 and~B.6), and the effective inequality of Bauer and Bennett gave the lower bound on $k_1$ (Proposition~B.7) and most of the case analysis of Lemma~4 (Appendix~\ref{app:cases}). Playing the window against the near miss for $x$ then gave Theorem~1, once it was noticed that Bauer and Bennett's unrounded exponent lies below the golden ratio.
The near-miss principle (Lemma~\ref{lem:near-misses}) extended this to Theorem~2, Corollaries~12 to~14, and Proposition~3.
The digit scan (Appendix~\ref{app:computations}) was computational evidence found along the way.
Theorem~4 is in Section~\ref{sec:ineffective}.
The census of known cycles and the heuristic for Conjecture~5 are in Section~\ref{sec:all-cycles}.
The final verification found and corrected a dropped constant in an intermediate step of Bauer and Bennett and confirmed their Proposition~8 for every $n_1 \ge 497$.
